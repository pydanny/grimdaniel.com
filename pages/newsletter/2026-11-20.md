In this 20th edition of the **Not Dead Yet Newsletter**, I talk about writing combat in 20th century sword and sorcery stories.

## Combat in 20th century sword and sorcery stories

As an adolescent in the late 1970s I began reading adult fantasy. That included Tolkien, but there were other authors. Two whose names I haven't forgetten were Robert E. Howard and Michael Moorcock, creators of the Conan and Elric. 

Of course as a youth I was entranced by the strength and toughness of Conan, who always made the right decision and leaned on luck. That made Elric so much more fascinating, he was weak both physically and morally and was always choosing the wrong path. Both characers had dramatic sword fights. Yes, there were issues even at age 13 that made me uncomfortable with both stories:

- Conan and the world around him is unabashedly racist 
- Elric use of drugs (eventually I realized its demonstration of his weakness)

In any case, recently I decided to read those stories again. This time from the perspective of an author with too much historical knowledge writing stories that contain combat. 

The first thing I noticed is that both Howard and Moorcock used the term "broadsword" in an apocryphal manner. This was common for the time, where nearly every non-short sword that wasn't a rapier or curved was labeled such. Broadswords historically were a Scottish basket-hilted weapon. My understand is that this pattern of mislabeling by authors began during late Victorian times.

Second, Conan absorbs damage like a video game character rather than a human. He gets cut and slashed, yet shrugs off injuries that would be mortal to us long enough for someone else to dab the blood from his skin. In a way that makes sense, Robert E. Howard was a boxer and not a swordsman. Taken in that context, Conan's fights make sense - he's trading punches with individuals and crowds, not sword strokes. The problem is that there's rarely any sense of lingering effect of weapons on Conan. Combined with his tendancy to not make close connections, to me that means there's never any feeling of risk in his stories. There's no sacrifice on his part. In reading his stories, the lack of serious consequences to Conan in battle makes the stories significantly less exciting.

Third, Elric's fights are about his mood. What he's feeling, usually rage, sadness, or desperation. In many combat scenes Elric experiences loss- there's usually stakes he needs to care about. Stormbringer is the loose cannon of the series, at the worst possible moments taking the lives (and souls) of people that Elric doesn't want to lose. It's no wonder that Elric eventually finds the strength and resolve to lock the dark sword away for years - but quickly returns to it rather than rely on drugs to keep him strong.

From a writer's perspective on combat, I personally enjoy the Elric stories more than Conan's.

## Everyone Dies

My latest book, Everyone Dies, has several combat sequences. Every moment where edged weapons are drawn serves a purpose, with stakes that have real meaning.
